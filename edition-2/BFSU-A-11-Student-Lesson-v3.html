    • Swirls or curls of color in Glass A: the water is moving (temperature differences or a bump). That's bulk flow. Note it in the table. It's a good talking point, not a failure.

    Adult cue: teaching note on the explanation

    The chapter also pictures dye particles bumping each other apart. This lesson leads with the counting explanation: every particle moves randomly, and more particles leave a crowded region than enter it. That reasoning works even when dye particles almost never meet, it matches the model's crossing counters, and it supports "motion continues once the mix is even." Both ideas point to the same cause: atomic and molecular motion.

    Bridge from Volume I · A-4

    What we already know about particles

    In Volume I (Lesson A-4 and later lessons) you built a particle model of matter. Before we use it, check it. Which of these fit that model?

      New words for old ideas. From now on we'll call these particles by their scientific names: atoms and molecules. A molecule is a group of two or more atoms joined together, like a water molecule. You don't need to know more about atoms yet.

      The big question

      Do atoms and molecules sit still like bricks in a wall, or do they jiggle about all the time? Make your case before you look at any evidence.

      Phenomenon

      The still-water glass

      Your two glasses of water have been sitting still while you worked. Soon you'll put one drop of food coloring into each glass. Nobody will stir Glass A, ever. (Glass B gets one gentle stir later, for comparison.)

      Predict Glass A: what will it look like at each time?

      Do it now: Hold the bottle just above the water and let one drop fall into the middle of each glass. Don't touch the glasses afterwards. Watch closely for 30 seconds, then go to the Diffusion table and press Stamp time on the "Start" row.
      Think about it, then open

      The drop falls and spreads across the bottom in seconds. That's not diffusion. Dye-water is denser (heavier for its size) than plain water, so the whole drop sinks as a lump. Moving as a lump like this is bulk flow. Our diffusion record starts once the dye has settled into a layer on the bottom.

      Evidence 1

      Brownian motion: the jiggle of visible specks

      Path A · With a microscope

      1. Stir about 1 teaspoon of whole milk into ½ cup of water.
      2. Put one small drop on a slide and lower a cover slip over it.
      3. Focus at low power, then switch to 400×. Turn the light down.
      4. Keep your hands off the microscope and wait about 30 seconds for any drifting to stop.
      5. Watch the tiniest round specks (milk-fat droplets).

      Path B · No microscope

      Simulation The model is a simulation: a computer following the rules scientists think are true. It helps you picture the idea, but it isn't evidence, because it can only show what it was programmed to do. Open it in Brownian mode with Show water molecules off first.

      Then turn the molecules on and watch what's really happening.

      Illustrative record, not real data What a scientist's record looks like. Around 1908, Jean Perrin watched single tiny grains under a microscope and marked each grain's position every 30 seconds, joining the dots with straight lines. The drawing below was made in the same style to show the format. It was generated for this lesson and is not a real measurement. Real records of this kind are evidence because a person actually observed and marked them.

      How to read it: each dot is where the grain was at one 30-second mark; the lines don't show the path in between (the real path zig-zags even more). A real record should look like this: no favored direction, and steps of very different sizes.

      Real jiggle or table bump? A bump jolts every speck at once, all in the same direction. Brownian motion is each speck jiggling on its own, all the time, even when nobody touches anything.

      How can we explain it?

      Read the scientists' explanation

      A fat droplet is made of millions of atoms. It's surrounded by water molecules, far too small to see, that are moving all the time and keep crashing into it. At any instant a few more hits land on one side than the other, so the droplet is nudged one way, then another, then another. You can't see the molecules, but you can see what their hits do.

      The jiggle is named after Robert Brown, who described it carefully in 1827. For decades no one could explain it. In 1905, Albert Einstein worked out how strongly moving molecules should jiggle a speck, and a few years later Jean Perrin measured specks under the microscope and found they moved just as predicted. That convinced most scientists that atoms and molecules are real.

      Why don't big things jiggle?

      In the model, switch the speck size from small to large. Then think about a desk, or the glass itself.

      Check your reasons
      1. More hits at once: a bigger object is hit by enormous numbers of molecules from every side at the same instant, so the pushes almost exactly cancel.
      2. More mass: a bigger object has more inertia, so whatever tiny push is left over barely moves it.

      The atoms inside a desk are jiggling too, but in every direction at once. For the desk to hop, trillions of them would all have to lurch the same way at the same moment. That essentially never happens.

      Evidence and its limits

      What this shows (from real observations only): Specks that were never alive (milk fat, ink, dust) jiggle endlessly with no push from outside. Something invisible must be moving and hitting them.

      What it can't show: We never see the molecules themselves, only the specks they hit. Bumps, drifting currents, or evaporation at the edge of the slide can make specks move too, so we have to rule those out.

      0 h

      Limit to notice: In perfectly still water, the colored layer grows only about 1–2 cm in the first day, then more and more slowly. Evening out a whole glass by diffusion alone would take months, because liquid particles are packed close and each dye particle gets bumped back and forth constantly. Real glasses often spread faster, because tiny temperature changes in a room set up gentle currents. So if your Glass A fills in faster than the "pure diffusion" glass, currents are helping. That's why the scientists' rule is no stirring, and why we measure, not just look. (The "gentle currents" glass is an illustration. Real currents vary from home to home.)

      Why does diffusion happen?

      Read the explanation

      Dissolved dye molecules are single molecules, even smaller than the specks in Brownian motion. Water molecules knock each one in a random direction, again and again. Picture a cluster of pool balls hammered from every side by fast marbles: the cluster gets broken up and scattered. Diffusion is what we see when molecular motion carries dissolved particles through a liquid or a gas.

      Random motion, but a definite direction?

      Here's the puzzle. Every particle moves randomly, so why does the color always spread from where there's more to where there's less, and never gather back into a blob?

      Turn on Track one particle and watch its path. Then watch the two crossing counters at the middle line.

      Check your reasoning

      The tracked particle wanders aimlessly: left, right, back again. It isn't heading anywhere. But where particles are crowded, more of them happen to wander out than wander in, simply because there are more of them there to move. So particles cross in both directions, and more cross from the crowded side. The overall result (the net movement) is spreading from more to less.

      Scientists say particles diffuse down a concentration gradient. That's a big-word way of saying "from where there's more to where there's less." A concentration gradient is just a gradual change from crowded to sparse.

      When the color is even, does diffusion stop? The net spreading stops, but the particles never do. Equal numbers now cross each way, so the color stays even. In the model, both counters keep climbing at the same rate.

      Decide for each case, then reveal. Think about how free the particles are to move among one another.

      Gases vs. liquids

      A perfume bottle opened across a room is smelled sooner than a dye drop colors a glass. Gas particles are far apart and not held together, so each one travels much farther before it hits anything. In liquids, particles slip and slide around each other but stay close together, so each dye particle is bumped back almost at once.

      Model limits: Real water molecules are about 3,000 times smaller than a 1-micrometer speck, and there are vastly more of them, packed tightly. The model is flat (2-D), shows far fewer particles, and runs much faster than real life. It shows the idea, not the real sizes or speeds.
      Biology

      Life depends on diffusion

      Every living thing has to get what it needs in and its wastes out. Pumping things takes energy, and energy is precious. Diffusion moves things for free, as long as there's a difference in concentration.

      Inside your lungs

      The windpipe branches again and again, like a tree, ending in hundreds of millions of tiny air sacs (alveoli), each wrapped in tiny blood vessels. The walls between air and blood are thin and moist.

      Check

      Your body uses oxygen, so blood arriving at the lungs has less oxygen than fresh air in the sacs. Oxygen diffuses from the air into the blood. Your body makes carbon dioxide as waste, so the blood has more carbon dioxide than the air in the sacs. Carbon dioxide diffuses out of the blood. Both happen at the same time, in opposite directions, each gas going from more to less. The only energy needed is for breathing, which keeps the air fresh, and for the heart, which keeps the blood moving. Together they keep both differences large.

      Think deeper

      Hints

      1: What happens to the difference in carbon dioxide between blood and air? 2: Think about the area of the lung surface, how fast diffusion can cross it, how fast the blood carries oxygen away, and how fast you can refresh the air.

      Plants

      In daylight, a leaf uses carbon dioxide to make food (photosynthesis), so the air inside the leaf has less carbon dioxide than the air outside. Carbon dioxide diffuses in through tiny pores. Photosynthesis also makes oxygen, so oxygen diffuses out. It's the same rule as our lungs, with the directions reversed.

      Fish

      Fish need oxygen too. Oxygen from the air diffuses into the water at the surface (a gas into a liquid). Then it diffuses from the water across the thin surfaces of the gills into the fish's blood.
