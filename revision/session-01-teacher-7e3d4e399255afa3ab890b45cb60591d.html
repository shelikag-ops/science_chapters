Start

Before class: what the learner does

  1. First reading: reread the chapter presentations already studied — A/B-1 Organising Things into Categories, A-2 Solids Liquids and Gases, A-4 Matter and Particles, A-5 Distinguishing Materials.
  2. Then, all required: Spectrum Science, Grade 3, Lesson 2.5 “Molecules on the Move” — p. 30 passage and all p. 31 workbook questions; Science: A Closer Look, Grade 2 — Chapter 9 pp. 294, 300, 308, 316 and Chapter 10 pp. 330–335; DK First Science Encyclopedia — pp. 4–13 and 52–61. If your edition’s page numbers differ, read the pages on the matching topics.
  3. Two paper-only thinking tasks: museum groupings (visitors vs packing); weather station material table.
  4. A different, later day: the 41-question written practice in the native Google Form — memory first, then revisit blanks.

All of the preparation above is required.

The private Form link is supplied separately; this deck does not collect or show the learner’s written-practice results.

Class activities

1. Learner retells the concepts · 10 min

The learner retells each chapter in their own words, without notes. Every topic gets a turn.

  1. BFSU A/B-1
    Tell me about sorting things into groups: what makes a good group, and why the same things can be grouped in different ways.
  2. BFSU A-2
    Tell me how you decide whether something is a solid, a liquid or a gas, and what can make it change.
  3. BFSU A-4
    Tell me what everything is made of, and what is going on inside a solid, a liquid and a gas.
  4. BFSU A-5
    Tell me how you would describe and choose materials for a job.

Follow up with “Tell me more” or “How do you know?”.

Class activities

2. Discussion of actual written-practice results · 20 min

  1. If the teacher has the learner’s results, the learner picks two questions they left blank or felt unsure about and explains their thinking now.
  2. Compare what was written from memory with what was added on revisit.
  3. Choose one idea to strengthen in the applications.
If no results are available, say so plainly and move on to the applications. Do not guess at results.
Application

3. Application 1 · Beach finds key · 7 min

A beach clean-up team wants a yes/no key so volunteers can sort finds quickly. These are the team’s observations:

FindMade by a living thing?Floats in seawater?Attracted to a magnet?Bends without breaking?
ShellYesNoNoNo
DriftwoodYesYesNoNo
KelpYesYesNoYes
Sea glassNoNoNoNo
Plastic bottle capNoYesNoYes
Rusty boltNoNoYesNo
Pumice stoneNoYesNoNo

Learner actions

  1. Choose a first yes/no question from the table that splits the finds into two groups.
  2. Keep adding yes/no questions until every find is on its own.
  3. Draw your key as a branching diagram.
  4. Name the observation that would be hardest for a volunteer to check on the beach, and suggest an easier one.

Response format: Branching key drawing + 1 sentence

About 7 minutes

Follow-up question: Your key puts pumice next to the bottle cap. Is that a mistake, or does it depend on what the key is for?
Application

3. Application 2 · Two puddles, two days · 7 min

Two puddles were made the same way (2 L of water on the same playground surface) on two different days. Measured conditions and puddle widths:

TimeDay 1: air 12 °C, cloudy, no windDay 2: air 24 °C, sunny, light wind
9:00120 cm120 cm
11:00110 cm80 cm
13:00100 cm35 cm
15:0090 cmonly a damp patch

Learner actions

  1. Describe the difference between the days, using numbers from the table.
  2. Where did the water go? Name the change of state.
  3. Draw a particle model (label it “model”) of water at the puddle’s surface on Day 2.
  4. Name two conditions that differ between the days, and explain why you cannot be sure which one caused the difference.

Response format: 2 sentences with numbers + 1 labelled model + 1 sentence on fairness

About 7 minutes

Follow-up question: If you wanted to find out whether wind matters, what would you keep the same?
Application

3. Application 3 · Mystery cubes · 6 min

Four cubes each measure 2 cm on every side. Their masses and a reference table are below.

CubeMassReferenceDensity
P4.0 gPine wood0.5 g/cm³
Q7.2 gPolypropylene plastic0.9 g/cm³
R21.6 gAluminum2.7 g/cm³
S63.2 gSteel7.9 g/cm³
Fresh water1.0 g/cm³
Very salty water1.2 g/cm³

Learner actions

  1. Find the volume of one cube; show your working.
  2. Calculate each cube’s density, with units.
  3. Match each cube to a material in the reference table.
  4. Predict which cubes float in fresh water and which in very salty water; explain one prediction.

Response format: Table: cube · density (with units) · material · fresh water · salty water

About 6 minutes

Follow-up question: Cube Q only just floats. Imagine adding a small steel washer to it. What would you need to know to predict what happens?
Application · optional

Optional alternative · The unlabeled powder · 6 min

A jar label fell off. The white powder could be table salt, sugar, sand or flour. An adult made these observations earlier (no tasting):

SampleUnder a magnifierStirred into room-temperature water
Table salt (reference)Tiny clear cube-shaped grainsDisappears; water stays clear
Sugar (reference)Clear grains, not cube-shapedDisappears; water stays clear
Sand (reference)Mixed-color irregular grainsGrains settle at the bottom
Flour (reference)Fine powder, no clear grainsWater turns cloudy
Mystery powderTiny clear cube-shaped grainsDisappears; water stays clear

Learner actions

  1. Which candidates can you rule out? Give the evidence for each.
  2. Which candidates are still possible after the water test alone?
  3. Which observation decides between them?
  4. Name one more safe test (no tasting) that could confirm your answer.

Response format: Ruled-out list with evidence · 1 deciding observation · 1 safe test

About 6 minutes

Follow-up question: Why is tasting never the test, even for powders found in a kitchen?

Optional: use instead of part of an application, never as extra work.

Class activities

4. Open-ended instant recall

No notes, no choices, no word lists. The learner answers out loud or in writing.

  1. Make up a new example of a category and say its rule.
  2. Explain how you would decide the state of something you have never seen before.
  3. Describe what the particles are doing when a solid melts.
  4. Pick one material and explain how its properties suit one job.
